1. For my baseline (every-frame FP32 PyTorch, batch 32), I ran two files (66 videos, about 5.2 h) on one Quadro RTX 6000. It took 3,833 s (63.9 min). I extrapolated this to 50 files (128.3 h) for about 26.1 hours. My final version finished 50 files (1,650 videos) in 7,058 s (1 h 57 min 38 s) and evaluated 22.3% of frames. On the 66-video benchmark, where I timed both, it took 310 s (12.4x faster). Adaptive sampling (`staged-1>3>all-VPN`) saved the most time. It cut 2,784 s (to 1,049 s, 3.65x) by evaluating 24.0% of frames in that benchmark.

2. `run.py` reads sorted Parquet files in a background thread. PyAV decodes MP4s with exact presentation timestamps relative to the first frame to assign seconds. It queues ordered YUV frames (~512 MB cap). With one-second look-ahead, the main thread evaluates each second's middle frame. If its middle-frame result differs from either neighbour's in object presence or people count, it expands to three evenly spaced frames. If their predictions disagree on object presence or people count, it evaluates remaining frames, each at most once. It converts selected frames to BGR, rotates tagged frames, letterboxes at 640, and runs same-video batches (<=32) on TensorRT FP16 YOLO11l (PyTorch fallback, conf 0.4, class-aware NMS IoU 0.7). `run.py` marks an object present if detected in at least half of a second's evaluated frames, takes the floor of the median per-frame people count, and writes one JSONL record per second (video ID, second index, objects, people count).

3. In my final version, the evidence points to the main thread's inference path. That path runs YUV-to-BGR conversion, batch preparation, TensorRT execution, and postprocessing. I did not profile the final TensorRT run per stage, so its slowest stage is still unknown. Earlier, I ran `validation/profile_run.py` on 10 fixed videos and synchronized CUDA around prediction to include GPU work. In the sampled FP32 profile, model forward took 256.0 of 338.4 s (75.6%). Decode took 31.8 s, YUV-to-BGR 26.3 s, preprocessing 10.6 s, and postprocessing 9.0 s. Under PyTorch FP16, forward dropped to 139 of 222.4 s (~63%). Before TensorRT, I tested extra FFmpeg decoder threads with PyTorch FP16 and background decoding, and saw almost no change. Then, in interleaved 66-video runs, I switched to TensorRT FP16. Wall time fell from ~535 s to 310 s (42% less), supporting the conclusion that the main thread limits throughput.

4. I built a separate every-frame FP32 YOLO11l reference with independent vote and median rules. My baseline matched it exactly on the 101 development videos. I then scored my optimized pipeline on those 101 videos and 48 untouched holdout videos. My final build reached dev/holdout object F1 (lower of micro/macro) 0.9928/0.9933 (brief >=0.95), exact people 99.22%/99.34% (>=85%), and within +/-1 99.98%/99.99% (>=95%). I verified that background decoding matched serial byte-for-byte on the same frames. All 17 fixtures and 23 unit tests passed. Separately, an independent packet-timestamp check verified 461,757 expected full-run second records, with zero missing, duplicate, or error lines.

5. Claude Code proposed testing FFmpeg frame threading in the PyAV background worker. Isolated decoding rose 4.9x (~3,900 to ~19,000 fps), but Claude noted the gain might disappear end to end. I tested five configurations on 10 fixed videos and required a 5% end-to-end gain. They took 191.9 to 193.8 s vs. controls 192.9 and 194.2 s, with byte-identical output and identical YOLO frames. The fastest was under 1% faster than the control mean, and some were slightly slower. I kept the defaults because decoding (~3,900 fps) outpaced pipeline use (~985 fps), so extra threads only filled the queue sooner.
